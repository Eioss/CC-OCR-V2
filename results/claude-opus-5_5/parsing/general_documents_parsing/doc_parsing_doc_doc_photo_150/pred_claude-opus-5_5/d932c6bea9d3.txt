\section*{False Positives and Positive Fallacies (p.~115) --- Summary}

The author describes testing positive for HIV. His doctor told him there
was only a 1-in-1,000 chance he was healthy, based on the statistic that
the test gives a positive result for uninfected blood in only 1 in 1,000
samples. As the author puts it, the doctor ``had confused the chances that
I would test positive \emph{if} I was not HIV-positive with the chances
that I would not be HIV-positive \emph{if} I tested positive.''

To correct the error, he applies Bayes's method:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the sample space: heterosexual, non-IV-drug-abusing white
        male Americans who have taken the test.
  \item Classify its members into true positives, false positives,
        true negatives, and false negatives.
  \item Count each class in an initial population of 10{,}000.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Figures given on the page}

\subsection*{Resulting Bayesian calculation}

\begin{align*}
\text{True positives} &\approx 1, \\
\text{False positives} &\approx \frac{1}{1000}\times 10{,}000 = 10, \\[4pt]
P(\text{HIV}^{+} \mid \text{test}^{+})
  &= \frac{\text{true positives}}{\text{true positives} + \text{false positives}}
   \approx \frac{1}{1+10} = \frac{1}{11} \approx 9\%.
\end{align*}

So a positive result implies roughly a 1-in-11 chance of infection,
not the 999-in-1,000 the doctor's reasoning suggested.
