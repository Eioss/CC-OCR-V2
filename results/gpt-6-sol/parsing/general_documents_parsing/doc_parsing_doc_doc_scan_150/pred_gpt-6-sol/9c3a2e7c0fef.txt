FRAGMENTS OF NORN. \hfill CXV

\textit{A proverbial Phrase.}
\end{center}

Da bomvilda vöga da vidla vöga pêsa (or passion) vöga.
Given with following explanation: \textit{Easter-Sunday weather will last
all the week.} More correct probably: \textit{the weather of Passion-week
will be the weather of Easter-week.}

The Coningsburgh phrase (acc. to Low): “Myrk in e Liora, Luce
in e Liunga, Tim in e Guest in e geungna” (It's dark in the Chim-
ney, but it's light thro' the Heath, it's still time for the stranger to
be gone).

Yell (in “de Herra”) this phrase is still preserved in the fol-
lowing form:

Mörka löra, lestra linga, tämra gstra gongara [myrkt er í ljöra,
ljóst er í lyngi, timi er at gestr(in) gengr]. With regard to the form
gøngara, cf. Fær. gongur.

Our Lord's Prayer\textsuperscript{1} from Foula (reported by Low in his “Tour
thro' Orkney and Shetland”, reprinted by Barry and in “Annaler for
nord. oldkyndighed” 1866):

Fy vor o er i Chimeri. Halagt vara nam det. La Konungdum
din cumma. La vill din vera gaerde i vridin sind eri Chimeri. Gav
vus dagh u daglight brau. Forgive sinder wasa (wara?) sin vi for-
giva gem ao sinda gainst wus. Lia wus eke o vera tempa, but de-
lira wus fro adu idu, for o in Konungdum, u puri, u glori. Amen.

\begin{center}
\textit{An Incantation.}
\end{center}

This is found in the tale: “Da Tiet i de Neen” (Shetland
Times 1879).

The tale itself, as well as the language used in it, doubtless or-
iginates from Al. (Wests.)

The formula, which is mainly in Scoto-English, has Norn words
sprinkled throughout, some of which old grammatical endings,
that have lost their significance. It runs as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l}
Da stuhl es scarp an fien,\\
Da sacheles emer a snean,\\
Da vird es sicer en pua,\\
A glimer i' mirk-as-dim hura,\\
La stuhl an vird ay gyrda\\
An prof er an skyla, an svinda.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent\textsuperscript{1} A man still living in Unst was formerly able, according to others'
statements, to recite Our Lord's Prayer in Norn, or at any rate, the greater part of
it. Some time ago, however, he had an illness which gradually enfeebled his mem-
ory, and when I looked him up, he could only remember the following corrupted
fragments: \ldots{} \textit{halagt wa'ts name} (hollowed be Thy name) — \textit{sa
timbilas} (as in Heaven).
