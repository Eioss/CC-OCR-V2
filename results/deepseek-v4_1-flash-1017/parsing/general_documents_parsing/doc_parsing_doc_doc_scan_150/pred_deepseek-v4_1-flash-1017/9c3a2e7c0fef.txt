\begin{center}
    \textit{A proverbial Phrase.}
\end{center}

Da dombvida voga da vidla voga pésa (or \textit{passion}) vóga. Given with following explanation: \textit{Easter-Sunday weather will last all the week. More correct probably: the weather of Passion-week will be the weather of Easter-week.}

The Conningsburgh phrase (acc. to Low): ``Myrk in e Lióra, Luce in e Liunga, Tim in e Guest in e geunga'' (It's dark in the Chimney, but it's light thro' the Heath, it's still time for the stranger to be gone).

In Yell (in ``de Herra'') this phrase is still preserved in the following form:

\begin{quote}
    Mörka lóra, lestra linga, tämra gestra gångara [myrkt er í ljóra, ljóst er í lyngi, tími er, at gestr(inn) gengr]. With regard to the form \textit{gångara}, cf. \textit{Fær. gongur}.
\end{quote}

Our Lord's Prayer\(^1\) from Foula (reported by Low in his ``Tour thro' Orkney and Shetland'', reprinted by Barry and in ``Annaler for nordisk oldkyndighed'', 1860):

\begin{quote}
    Fy vor o er i Chimeri. Halaght vara nam det. La Konungdum din cumma. La vill din vera guerde i vrildin sinda eri Chimeri. Gav vus dagh u dagloght brau. Forgive sindor wara (wara?) sin vi forgiva gem ao sinda gainst wus. Lia wus ikè o vera tempa, but de-livra vo fro adlu idru, for do i Konungdum, u puri, u glori. Amen.
\end{quote}

\begin{center}
    \textit{An Incantation.}
\end{center}

This is found in the tale: ``Da Tief i' de Neean'' (Shetland Times 1879).

The tale itself, as well as the language used in it, doubtless originates from Al. (West.).

The formula, which is mainly in Scoto-English, has Norn words sprinkled throughout, some of which show old grammatical endings, that have lost their significance. It runs as follows:

\begin{quote}
    Da stuil es scarp an fien,\\
    Da sacheles emer a snean,\\
    Da vird es sicer en pura,\\
    A glimer i' mirk-as-dim hura,\\
    La stuil an vird ay gyrda\\
    An prof er an skyla, an svirda.
\end{quote}
