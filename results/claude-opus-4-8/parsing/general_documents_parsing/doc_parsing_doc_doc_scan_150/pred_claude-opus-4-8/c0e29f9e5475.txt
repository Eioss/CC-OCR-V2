\noindent\underline{Introduction.}\\
The language FEA was developed to facilitate semantic information retrieval on litera\=
ture about abstract algebra. \quad FEA has since proved useful as a means of stating program
assertions, and may be used also as a non-procedural programming language.

\noindent The paper is divided into four sections:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An informal introduction to FEA
    \item The possible use of FEA in information retrieval
    \item The possible use of FEA in program validation
    \item The formal definition of FEA.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\underline{1. \quad An informal introduction to FEA.}\\
In this section the language FEA -- Formal English for Algebra (or Assertions) is described
informally. \quad References to the formal definition in section 4 is given in the form
(F20 - F22) which refers to the productions numbered F20, F21, F22.

\bigskip
\noindent FEA is not a programming language. \quad It is a formal, unambiguous language that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can be understood by mathematically mature persons.
    \item Can be used to paraphrase theorems, hypotheses, axioms, rules of inference and
    (algebraic) facts. \quad These paraphrases are called stellings. \quad (The word stellings
    is coined to ensure that no confusion with the term `proposition', which is used in
    logic, can arise).
    \item Can, in certain cases, be used to construct algorithms automatically.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The syntax and most of the semantics of FEA is defined in terms of PLISP. \quad PLISP is a
high level language for LISP, and is the subject of a paper in preparation. \quad The PLISP
that is used in this paper is effectively self-explanatory, but notes will be added
wherever confusion may arise.

\bigskip
\noindent\underline{Elementary Atoms} (F42 - F45): \quad An atom is a predicate applied to one or more terms.
A term is a variable, or is a function of one or more terms. \quad The syntax of a term
will depend on the system in which FEA is embedded. \quad In this \textbf{paper} a term is any
PLISP expression.\\
E.g. \quad The following are $\langle$atom$\rangle$'s:
\begin{align*}
    &\text{X EQUALS Y} + 1\\
    &\text{B} \in \text{S2} \cup \text{S3}\\
    &\text{T3} \subset \text{T25} \cap \text{T15}\\
    &10 > \text{N} + \text{J} - 6
\end{align*}

\noindent In general the notation of formal logic is allowed in FEA. \quad This means that \quad $\langle$atom$\rangle$'s
can be written in the form: \quad $\langle$predicate$\rangle$ \quad ($\langle$termlist$\rangle$)\\
E.g. \quad REAL(X)
\begin{align*}
    &\text{BETWEEN (X; K; W)}\\
    &\text{IS-RING (T; S; o+; ox)}\\
    &\text{SUBSETP (B} \cap \text{S;S} \cup \text{B)}
\end{align*}

\noindent Other forms of $\langle$atom$\rangle$'s that correspond closer to a natural language are also allowed
in FEA.

\bigskip
\noindent\underline{Elementary Quantified Formulae} (F1 - F3): \quad A quantified well formed formula is a well
formed formula with zero or one or more quantifiers preceding it.

\bigskip
\noindent A quantified well formed formula, or $\langle$qwff$\rangle$ , is the basic unit in a FEA stelling.
